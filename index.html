About me

Who I am

I’m 2three1y: a developer who likes understanding how technology works, then shaping it into something people can actually use. I’m drawn to projects with a little personality, whether that means a playful web experiment, a useful browser tool, or an audio experience that brings a favorite era of tech back to life.

I think of myself as a sovereign architect: I care about who controls our tools, where our data lives, and whether technology gives people more agency rather than less. That outlook informs Looscid, my evolving exploration of a local-first, decentralized workspace—not a promise that one project can replace everything, but a practical way to ask what a more user-controlled web could look like.

Accessibility is part of how I design and build, not a finishing pass. I value clean semantic HTML, clear headings and landmarks, keyboard-friendly interactions, visible focus, readable content, and interfaces that communicate their state. The web works better when its structure is understandable to people and assistive technology alike.

Outside the architecture and code, I’m a technology enthusiast with a soft spot for audio: classic voices, synths, system sounds, and the small details that make old hardware and software memorable. I like bringing that curiosity into projects while keeping the experience straightforward, accessible, and a bit fun.

Accessibility advocacy

Building for everyone

Accessibility is not an optional extra to me. I care about semantic HTML, visible focus, keyboard support, high contrast, readable structure, screen-reader navigation, and interfaces that do not hide important information behind unnecessary interaction.

Looscid

An autonomous local-first workspace

Looscid is an autonomous, local-first decentralized web workspace built around digital sovereignty. It is an evolving operating environment for keeping data and daily tools useful on the local device, rather than presenting itself as a finished replacement for iOS or Android.

The technical focus is screen-reader ergonomics: predictable landmarks and headings, short discrete blocks, keyboard-first movement, and status changes exposed through aria-live messaging. The goal is a workspace that feels calm and navigable when every interaction is being spoken.
